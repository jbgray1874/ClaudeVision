The one that changes the shape of the problem — on-premise vision

Qwen3.8-27B · Apache-2.0 · released 14 Aug 2026

Why this is first and not buried under the frontier models

Every drawing-vision call the engine makes today leaves the building and costs money per page. That is a per-job cost, a per-job latency, and a standing question about sending customer drawings to a third party. Qwen3.8-27B is the first open-weight model that plausibly removes all three at once: Apache-2.0 licence, a built-in vision encoder, and small enough to run on one graphics card.

SDI already runs its own tin. This is the kind of thing that fits how the business is set up, not a subscription bolted onto it.

What it isSecondary
27–28B denseVision encoder 262K native contextApache-2.0

Announced 3 August alongside Qwen3.8-Max, weights published 13–14 August on Hugging Face under Apache 2.0. A dense 27B model with hybrid attention and — the part that matters for us — a vision encoder, so it reads images, not just text. Native 262K context, reported extensible to 1M.

Reported benchmarks put it at 61.7 on SWE-bench Pro and 52 on the Artificial Analysis Intelligence Index, a 14-point jump over Qwen3.6-27B. Simon Willison's write-up is positive but flags that it defaults to wildly overthinking things — worth knowing before you measure latency.

What it would take to run here

Full BF16 weights are around 55.6 GB. Quantised to 4-bit it fits a 24 GB card — an RTX 3090 or 4090. Reported throughput on modest hardware: 24.5 tokens/sec on a Ryzen AI Max+ 395, 51.8 tokens/sec on a Radeon AI PRO R9700 with 32 GB.

That is one workstation-class card, not a server rack. Against the current estimating box — which already needs a machine capable of running SOLIDWORKS COM — this is an incremental purchase rather than a new class of infrastructure.

Where it would slot into the engine
Source rank 40Drawing visionNote scan

The price chain currently puts vision at rank 40 — the weakest source, used to fill gaps the deterministic readers leave. That is exactly the right place to trial a local model: the arbitration already treats its output as low-confidence and will not let it override a DXF or a title-block read.

The honest test: take the 10575-02 pack, run the same pages through the current vision path and through a local Qwen3.8, and compare against the manual estimate. If it holds, the per-job vision cost goes to zero and the drawings stop leaving the network. If it does not, we have lost a day and learned the ceiling.

The caveats, stated plainly

Everything above is from secondary sources. Benchmark figures, quantised memory footprints and throughput numbers all come from third-party write-ups, not from a vendor page. Confirm against the official model card before buying hardware.

A 27B model is not Opus 5. It will be worse at ambiguous drawings, and the failure mode of a local model is the same as a hosted one — a confident wrong answer. Rank 40 and the credibility gate are what make trialling it safe.

Running your own inference is a job. Model updates, driver updates, and somebody who owns it. That is a real cost against "no per-call fee", and on a team where one developer is already the constraint it deserves saying out loud.

Frontier models — what actually shipped

Mid-August was the densest release window of the year

Claude Opus 524 Jul 2026
$5 / $25 per 1M1M context 128K outputEffort toggle

Anthropic's near-frontier model: roughly Fable-5-level capability at half Fable's price, same sticker as Opus 4.8. API string claude-opus-5. 1M context both default and maximum; 128K output in standard requests, up to 300K through the Batches API with a beta header.

The new thing is a low / medium / high effort toggle per request — you trade cost against capability call by call rather than picking a model tier up front. For the engine that maps cleanly: low effort for high-volume classification, high effort for the handful of parts that decide whether an estimate is right.

Why this is the cheapest change on the page: _call_anthropic_llm already exists in web_ai_price_lookup.py and the key is already configured. This is a model-string change, not an integration.

CAD and design tooling — inside the licence we already hold

SOLIDWORKS 2026 shipped three AI companions. One of them overlaps the roadmap directly.

F1 may already exist

The technical design roadmap carries F1 · PDF → ASM/PRT reconstruction as POC pending, with dates to be confirmed — rebuilding 3D parts and assemblies from PDF drawings, described as the furthest-reaching item on the list.

LEO does this today. It converts a 2D drawing or PDF into an editable parametric model, and it is shipping in SOLIDWORKS 2026 SP1 through the SOLIDWORKS Labs tab in the Task Pane. Before any work is scoped against F1, somebody should open that tab on a seat we already pay for and try it on a real legacy drawing. If it works even partially, F1 changes from a build to an evaluation.

LEO — the engineer's companionVendor
SW2026 SP1 / FD01Labs tabAgentic

Reverse-engineers BREP bodies and 2D drawings into parametric models. Generates manufacturable parts from sketch inputs. Builds assemblies from natural-language prompts. Auto-generates drawings — with control over sheet sizes, drawing standards and which template to use. Sets up SIMULIA Abaqus simulations from a prompt. Will attempt to rebuild a single-body STEP or IGES as an editable parametric part.

Overlaps two roadmap items, not one. The PDF-to-parametric work is F1. The drawing generation overlaps C3 · Drawing generation from ASM/PRT, which is already POC-complete in-house and waiting on a licence. Worth understanding which of the two SDI would rather own, because they are not both needed.

Build notes for the in-house PDM

P3.1 starts 8 September. One known problem is worth designing around now, not in October.

SolidWorks files churn at the binary level — every open and save

The single most repeated warning in the CAD-version-control literature is this: a SOLIDWORKS assembly changes at the binary level every time it is opened and saved, even if nothing was moved.

For a PDM built on GitLab and LFS that has a direct consequence. Every open-and-close produces a new LFS object of full file size, with no meaningful diff. Storage grows with activity rather than with change, and history fills with revisions that record nothing. On a vault of assemblies that compounds quickly.

This is a design question for P3.1, not a defect. It is answerable — content-hash the meaningful payload rather than the file, or gate commits on an explicit check-in rather than on save, or prune unreferenced LFS objects on a schedule. But it is far cheaper to answer in the MVP than to retrofit once there is a year of vault history.

Prior art worth an hourSecondary

Not as alternatives to building — the in-house decision is made — but because each has met the same problems and their documentation says how they landed:

  • Anchorpoint — Git plus LFS positioned explicitly as a lightweight SOLIDWORKS PDM alternative for smaller teams. Closest thing to the architecture P3.1 describes.
  • DocDokuPLM and openPLM — open-source PLM. Heavier than needed, but their data models are worth reading before designing ours.
  • lakeFS, git-annex, ArtiVC — large-file versioning approaches other than Git LFS, if the churn problem above bites harder than expected.
  • SVN with TortoiseSVN — the old answer. Reliable, no CAD intelligence, manual link management. Useful mainly as the baseline the new thing has to beat.

→ Before P3.1 development starts on 16 Sep: read how Anchorpoint handles binary churn

What this does not change

The case for building rather than buying is unchanged and is worth restating, because a landscape report is exactly where somebody reasonably asks "why are we writing our own?".

The in-house PDM is being built with a Document Manager intelligence layer over PostgreSQL — it knows what is inside the files, not just that they changed. Nothing on the list above does that, and it is the part that feeds the estimating engine. A generic version-control product would solve check-in and check-out and leave the reason we are doing this untouched.

Estimating-adjacent software — what the market has, and what it does not

Worth knowing what exists before anyone asks why we built our own

The AI takeoff marketSecondary
Togal.AIQuotr.aiTheTakeoff.AIAutodesk Takeoff

A real and maturing category: computer vision reading drawing sets and pulling priced quantities automatically. Togal.AI classifies rooms and measures footprints from floor plans. Quotr.ai claims 95–99% vector accuracy feeding a live cost matrix. TheTakeoff.AI reads a full drawing set and pulls line lists, valve counts, instrument counts. Autodesk Takeoff does quantities from 2D sheets and 3D models inside Construction Cloud.

All of it is construction. Floor plans, rooms, valves, instruments — quantities from architectural and MEP drawing sets. None of it does sheet-metal flat patterns, bend counts, weld sequences, powder coverage by mass, or a five-tier price chain against a company's own historical job corpus.

What that tells us

Two things, and they point in opposite directions.

The approach is validated. Reading drawings with vision and producing priced quantities is not speculative — there is a competitive market doing it at scale in an adjacent industry. That is reassuring about the direction.

Nobody is coming to do ours. The gap between "measure a floor plan" and "cost a laser-cut, folded, welded and powder-coated display unit against 1,982 historical jobs" is the whole product. No vendor in that list is going to close it, which means the build-versus-buy question does not really exist for us — but it also means the accuracy work is ours alone.

Research worth trackingSecondary

Academic work on fine-tuned document-understanding transformers for parsing engineering drawings into structured information is active and published. This is closer to our problem than the construction takeoff vendors are — parsing a title block, a BOM table and a revision block into fields is exactly what extractor_patterns.py does with regexes today.

Not an action. A direction: if the deterministic readers plateau, a small fine-tuned document model over SDI's own drawing corpus is the route that does not depend on any vendor.

The wider SOLIDWORKS marketplace — what is buyable rather than buildable

Partner add-ins and native tools that overlap the technical design roadmap

An add-in already batch-exports DXF with bend lines

The estimating engine's largest single source of inaccuracy is drawings arriving without flat patterns. CADshift is a SOLIDWORKS add-in that batch-exports laser-ready DXF files with bend lines, metadata and BOM quantities — which is the output C1 was built in-house to produce.

It does not dodge the licence problem. An add-in runs inside SOLIDWORKS and needs a seat, exactly as C1 does. So this is not a way around the constraint. What it might be is a way to stop maintaining that capability ourselves — the seat gets resolved once and then the flat-pattern export is somebody else's product to keep working.

SOLIDWORKS Costing — worth understanding before anyone else finds it

Dassault ship a costing tool in the box. Somebody will eventually ask why SDI built an estimating engine when SOLIDWORKS does costing, so it is worth having the answer ready.

SOLIDWORKS Costing prices one modelled part from its geometry against a rate template. It needs a model. It does not read a PDF pack, does not resolve a GA-to-sub-assembly-to-detail BOM tree, does not price against 1,982 historical SDI jobs, does not carry a five-tier source waterfall with confidence per line, and does not mirror the SDI estimating workbook to the cell.

It is a calculator; the engine is an estimator. Both true, and the distinction only sounds defensive if it has not been said in advance.

The honest read on buy-versus-build here

Nothing in this table replaces the estimating engine. Several things in it overlap C1 and C3, which are POC-complete in-house and licence-blocked.

That is the interesting question, and it is Yogesh's to answer rather than mine: given four finished tools waiting on a seat, is the flat-pattern export worth owning, or is it worth buying so the effort goes to the parts nobody sells — the PDM, the DXF generation from a GA where no model exists, and the estimating integration?

Nobody sells that last one. Which is roughly the argument for buying the first.

Inside the estimating engine — the tooling layer

Not models. The libraries and approaches the pipeline is built out of.

Paperless Parts — the closest thing to a competitorSecondary
62 geometric attributesSheet metal specific $300–750 / month

Unlike the construction takeoff vendors, this one is actually in our industry. It detects 62 geometric attributes from a 3D CAD model, unfolds parts to get dimensions and thickness, and identifies cuts, bends, pierces, cut length and etchings as costing variables. It quotes assemblies including underlying components and purchased hardware as a single line rather than part by part. Growth tier $300/month, Professional $750/month.

Read that list again against our own faults. Bends, cut length, unfolding, assemblies-with-bought-ins — that is the same problem space, solved commercially.

And here is why it does not solve ours. It prices from a 3D CAD model. SDI's defining problem is the packs that arrive without models or flat patterns — which is why F1 and C1 exist at all. And it prices against its own rate configuration, not against 1,982 historical SDI jobs, the UDEF anchor, PMA_TBL, or the SDI workbook it has to mirror to the cell.

Worth knowing it exists, and worth being able to say precisely why we are not buying it.

Document AI has moved past pdfplumberSecondary
SuryaLLMWhispererCamelot

The category now calls itself Agentic Document Processing — multimodal models plus layout awareness plus semantic reconstruction, rather than rule-based parsing.

The distinction that matters to us: tools that dump table data versus tools that produce structured, labelled output — knowing that one column holds part numbers, another quantities, another unit prices. That is precisely what bom_table_extractor.py is trying to do with rules.

The specific gap: pdfplumber and Camelot work well on digital PDFs with visible borders and fail on scanned documents and borderless tables. Layout-model tools like Surya and LLMWhisperer handle both. If any part of the BOM-table extraction is imperfect on customer-supplied or scanned packs, that is the shape of the fix — not a better regex.

→ When a BOM table reads badly, check first whether it is scanned or borderless

The architecture is already the recommended oneNo change needed

The current best-practice advice for document extraction is a hybrid: rule-based parsers for structured, predictable fields, and models reserved for free-form content and edge cases where rigid rules fail.

That is the engine. Deterministic readers hold ranks 70–90, vision sits at rank 40 filling gaps, and the arbitration rule is fill-a-gap-always, replace-a-stronger-source-never.

Worth stating plainly because it is the sort of thing that gets questioned in a year when somebody suggests replacing the readers with a model. The industry consensus is that this would be the wrong move, and we already do the right one.

What is deliberately not in this report

Nothing here proposes changing the engine's architecture, and nothing proposes replacing a deterministic reader with a model. The price chain works because steps 1 to 6 are reproducible and step 7 is flagged; every item above either sits at rank 40 where it cannot override a stronger source, or is a like-for-like model-string swap. The interesting technology this month does not change that rule, and should not.
