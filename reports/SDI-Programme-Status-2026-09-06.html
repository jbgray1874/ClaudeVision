What moved since 27 August

The PDM dates moved right, and it is not a slip. Yogesh's tracker now interleaves each tool's deployment days inside the next tool's build, so no build occupies a day that is booked for a go-live. P2 goes from 24 to 29 Sep; the four PDM phases move by one to three weeks and the last lands on 1 Feb 2027, which is why the axis above now runs to February. P1 on 15 Sep is unchanged.

Three go-lives inside a fortnight, not four inside five weeks. P1 on 15 Sep, Drawing Search on 25 Sep, P2 on 29 Sep. The estimating engine keeps its September / October target, and the thing that decides it is now visible: reviews returned, at five jobs a week.

One line stopped. Client Briefing is on hold from 4 September; its bar ends there. Nothing else depends on it, and it is not deleted — see Workstream 3 for who picks it up and in what form.

Deterministic cost-estimating engine — a CAD pack in, a costed workbook on the estimators' own template out, with a covering e-mail, a job report and a customer quote. Every priced line names its source. Since 27 August: 156 commits, and the test suite stands at 4,212 passing.

Five jobs a week, and the loop that was missing

The 19 August report's problem was that runs went out and nothing came back. The parallel run now has a cadence — five jobs ran last week, five more are queued for this week — and the run itself closes the loop: the engine e-mails the finished estimate to the people who asked for it, with the explanation attached, the moment it completes.

One of five returned

Tim's review of 12349-02 is the most useful single piece of feedback the programme has had: it was line by line, and it turned three engine defects into three fixes in a day. The other four are with Dave's team. The go-live target is decided on this number, so the ask of estimating this week is the same as the run rate: five out, five back.

What landed in the engine

The finished estimate is e-mailed, automaticallyNew

The estimating page carries a recipients box pre-filled from a saved list. When a run completes, the workbook, report and covering note go to those people with no developer in the loop. The customer quote is withheld while an estimate is provisional — it is the one document written to be read by a customer, and it must not leave with a figure nobody has stood behind.

Every pound explained, against the sheet's own totalsNew

An AI Explanation tab in the workbook, the same text as section 14 of the job report and the covering e-mail. It sums every material block and every labour row against the Estimate sheet's totals and states any difference in pounds and in a direction. Labour is split into set-up and run, with what it does at 1, 10, 25, 50, 100 and 250 off. The lines that still need a person are listed worst-first with the code, the money at stake and the drawing page — not counted.

Why it matters. "3 lines carry no price" is true and unactionable. This is what an estimator needs to finish the sheet in minutes rather than an afternoon.

Parity, from the portal, on every runRebuilt

The parity harness had run once, in July. It was measuring the wrong things in five ways at once. Rebuilt: both workbooks are read by the one block reader, each side is reconciled to its own totals before anything is compared, differences are signed both ways, and the inputs behind a money gap — blank, gauge, quantity, nest — are reported with it. It runs from the estimating page whenever a manual sheet is attached, and files a ledger row per job.

One estimate, every quantity asked forNew

"1, 50, 100, 250 and 500 is the norm now." The estimate runs once at the first quantity and the workbook is recalculated at each of the others, one file per quantity, with the order-level freight divided correctly rather than a pallet landed on each of 500 units.

A run is measured, not guessed atDone

Every phase of a run is timed and printed slowest-first, and a machine profile samples memory and CPU during a live estimate. Those measurements — 38 GB peak, about four threads, roughly forty minutes a job — are the figures the workstation in section 05 is sized from.

This week's first job is through pre-flightIn progress

7332-01, a plated A3 signage stand for Harrods, exercised three things the earlier jobs had not: a subcontract plating line on a welded assembly, a bent square-tube leg, and a felt pad with no part code. All three now price and say where the figure came from. A ten-second pre-flight tool proves the route and the leg's length before the forty-minute run is spent. Reviewable, not yet releasable — plating membership and the tube length are Tim's calls, and the sheet says so.

Why the dates moved, and why that is the honest version

The 27 August dates assumed each tool's deployment took no developer time. It does: deployment and go-live are hands-on days, and with one developer they have to be carved out of the next tool's build. The tracker now slots them in explicitly — two days for P2, two to three for each PDM phase — and the plan reports no clashes. Leave from 10 December to 4 January is unchanged and is what pushes P3.3 and P3.4 into the new year.

The lever is still a second pair of hands. Every date from P3.1 onwards depends on one person being available.

Watch-points, in Yogesh's words

  • The Design Vault proof of concept needs a Linux / GitLab machine by 10 September. That is the shared workstation in section 05. If it is not ready, he switches to the design-generation work (PDF / DXF / IGES) scheduled later, so nothing stalls.
  • Once the Vault is live the W: drive stops being the single source of truth. Proposed: sync W: on released status. Raised at the daily AI meeting on 3 September with the need for backups and proper Dev / UAT / Prod environments.
  • Network scan speed over Wi-Fi is too slow — under investigation, and answered structurally by putting the workstation beside the file server.

Estimating engine · J. Gray

Running manually on a laptop today. Needs 38 GB and the fastest single core available: SolidWorks and Excel are single-threaded, and it drives both through an interactive Windows desktop.

Drawing Search · M. Yazir

Must be always-on and beside the file server. A 16 GB SolidWorks-certified GPU takes an image search from 9.5 s to 1.1 s; over VPN, indexing takes days rather than hours.

Design Vault POC · Y. Kumar

Two Ubuntu guests for GitLab and PostgreSQL under Hyper-V, with a Windows Server guest later. Starts 10 September.

The shape, in one line each

  • CPU — AMD Ryzen 9 9950X: the fastest single core, not the most cores.
  • Memory — 128 GB as 2 × 64 GB ECC, two slots free to reach 256 GB. Measured need is 106–114 GB across the three.
  • GPU — 16 GB SolidWorks-certified card; the one line Muhammad's requirement turns on.
  • Always-on — mirrored NVMe system disks, a 4 TB data disk for the Git LFS volume, and a UPS, because it runs unattended overnight for three teams.
  • Windows 11 Pro on the metal, Ubuntu as Hyper-V guests. Not Windows Server, because SolidWorks is unsupported on it.

Two decisions needed before the order goes

1. Can it sit on the same LAN as sdi-dc01? IT. Free, and the single most important line in the request. Over VPN the file server is 226 ms away and indexing crawls; on the same network the same job takes hours. It cannot be fixed later by buying more machine.

2. Is AD integration for Drawing Search firm, or nice-to-have? Muhammad. SolidWorks cannot run on Windows Server, so the search would run under Windows 11 Pro without AD group control. If AD is a hard requirement the host OS choice reopens.

Not in this budget, flagged for later: a 256 GB memory upgrade (~£500) when the Phase 4 Windows worker arrives, and a Windows Server licence, CALs and a second SolidWorks seat at the same point. Supersedes the single-purpose server of 25 August, which sized for one workload rather than three.
